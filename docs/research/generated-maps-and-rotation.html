Generated maps,
and how often they turn over

How to generate maps that are provably worth playing, how to seed and verify them for nothing, and what the rotation cadence actually trades away — which is not what it looks like.

Third of three. Companion to the skill audit and the planning note. It decides nothing; the cadence is an open question in vision §10.

Bottom line

The standard method for generating good levels is to make a lot of them and score each one by simulation — and the standing objection to it in the literature is that simulating every candidate is too slow. That objection does not apply here. At 2.75 ms a match, this project can afford the expensive version of the technique that everyone else has to approximate, and it can score maps on the only property that matters: whether good plans disagree about them.

The cadence question looks like freshness versus staleness. It is not. It is a fight between the map and the ghost pool, because a pool indexed by (map, stage) empties every time the map turns over — and the pool is what the async mode is. That, not solvability, is what should set the number.

1What rotation actually buys

A hard map delays solving. A map nobody has seen prevents it. Those are different goods and the design needs both, because they fail at different times: geometry that resists a solution still yields to a month of collective attention, and novelty with no depth underneath produces a map that is fresh and immediately boring.

Rotation earns its place here for a reason specific to this design rather than a general one. The skill audit found that fun in this genre is pattern mastery, and that a game dies at the moment its pattern is fully learned. Most games spread that learning across weeks using an unlock ramp; the vision's nothing persists rule deletes that ramp entirely. A rotating map is the only renewable source of new pattern this design has left — the variance anchors reset every run, the roster is fixed, and the opponent pool is made of the same players making the same decisions.

The precedent to copy is Slay the Spire's daily. Its structure is exactly the one proposed here: one seed, identical for every player worldwide, rotating every twenty-four hours, with its own scoreboard. The reason it works is not novelty — it is that a shared seed is what makes a scoreboard mean anything. Everyone navigated the same decisions, so the ranking is about the decisions. That property is worth more here than it is there, because the histogram design depends on comparing results that are actually comparable.

Which yields the first constraint, and it is not optional: everyone in a cycle must be on the same map. The both-boards match structure resolves your wave against their defense and theirs against yours — two resolutions on two different geometries are not a match, they are two unrelated events with one scoreboard.

2The generator

The established method is search-based procedural content generation. It needs three parts — a representation, a search algorithm, and a fitness function — and it works by generating a large volume of candidates, scoring each, and using the score to steer the next generation. It is not novel and that is the point: it is the thing the field settled on.

The finding this note exists for

The literature's standing objection to simulation-based fitness is the one problem this project does not have.

Scoring a generated level by playing it is agreed to be the best signal and rejected as too expensive — the surveys say plainly that the major drawback of simulation-based fitness is running time, and the workarounds are proxies: train a classifier to guess whether a level is playable, score on cheap structural heuristics, simulate only the survivors.

This project's match costs 2.75 ms. A candidate map swept against a hundred plans is under a second. A thousand candidates is a coffee. The proxy is unnecessary; the real signal is affordable — and the harness that produces it is already on the build order at step 4 for an unrelated reason.

The fitness function is the interesting half

Structural metrics are the obvious choice and they are all proxies for the thing actually wanted. Path length correlates with time under fire; chokepoint count correlates with where damage concentrates; elevation variance correlates with how much the range lever matters. Every one of them is a guess about play, and every one can be satisfied by a map that is structurally rich and strategically trivial.

The direct measurement is available instead, and it is the one the planning note calls D7:

Sweep many good plans against a candidate map and report the spread of outcomes across them. A map where every competent plan scores the same is solved. A map where competent plans diverge has decisions in it.

The definition of "far less solvable", written down

That is a fitness function with no proxy in it. It also has a second reading worth keeping: a map where outcomes spread too widely is not deep, it is random — so the target is a band rather than a maximum, and the same sweep reports both ends.

Generate an archive, not a map

The refinement worth taking is quality-diversity, and specifically MAP-Elites. Instead of searching for the single best map, it discretises a behaviour space into bins and keeps the best map found in each — so the output is an archive of maps that are all good and all different, spread deliberately across the axes you chose to care about.

For this project the behaviour axes almost name themselves: path length, branch count, elevation spread, chokepoint concentration. Fill the archive once, offline, and the rotation becomes a scheduler drawing from it. That matters for a reason beyond variety, and it is the subject of §5.

3Seeding, for nothing

The seeding requirement turns out to be mostly already met, which is the happiest finding in this note. Four pieces exist in sim/ today and they compose into exactly what generated rotation needs.

What existsWhat it givesStatus
HexMap.FromCells Builds a grid from a byte array with no file behind it — so a generator can hand a map straight to a match Have it
Match(…, ulong seed) A seed already threads through the constructor, under the one-RNG-stream rule Have it
HexMap.MapHash A hash over the parsed grid. A client's claim about which map it played is checkable Have it
GhostRecord.MapHash + MapHandle A stored defense already says which map it was on, by hash and by handle. The format anticipated this Have it
The generator itself seed → cells, a pure function, no filesystem, deterministic across platforms To build
A simcli mode Turn a seed into a map, and a map into a swept score, from a shell To build

So a map becomes a seed rather than an asset, and three consequences follow that are worth stating separately because each removes a cost somebody would otherwise pay:

  • A map costs a handful of bytes in a record. Not a blob, not a file reference, not a content pipeline. The ghost pool's storage story — hundreds of bytes per defense — survives rotation untouched.
  • Anti-cheat still falls out for free. The server re-simulates a claimed result; with the map derived from a seed and verified by MapHash, "I played a different map" is not available as a lie. The existing guarantee extends with no new machinery.
  • The generator version becomes content, and must be hashed like content. This is the trap. A record identifies a map by hash, but a *seed* only identifies a map relative to a generator — so changing the generator silently changes what a seed means. The existing discipline already covers this shape of problem (the content hash folds parsed integers; format versions are per record kind), and the generator has to join it rather than be an exception to it.

One hard requirement inherited from the ADRs: the generator must be as deterministic as the simulation. Same seed, same bytes, on every platform and architecture the determinism matrix covers. That rules out floating point in the generator entirely — the same rule sim/ already lives under, and the same reason.

4The cadence, and the pool

Daily or weekly looks like a question about how fast a map gets solved. It is not, or rather that is the cheap half. The expensive half is that the ghost pool is indexed by (map, stage), so every rotation empties it at every stage simultaneously.

What each end actually costs

PropertyFast rotation (daily)Slow rotation (weekly+)
Solving Effectively prevented — nobody has time A week is enough for a community to converge on a line
Ghost pool Cold every day at every stage, forever Fills — the cold start happens once per map
Comparability Strong — one map, one cohort, one histogram Stronger still, since the sample is seven times deeper
Mastery Nothing map-specific is learnable; skill is general only Map knowledge becomes a real skill with a shelf life
Archive size 365 maps a year, all disposable 52 a year — a generator run is a year of content
Missing a day You missed a map entirely Forgiving; suits a friend group with jobs
Recommendation, weakly held

Weekly for the round-robin, with a daily as a separate mode rather than a replacement.

The pool argument dominates at friend scale, and it dominates hardest at exactly the moment the game is newest — a daily rotation with three players is a permanently empty pool, which is the cold-start problem made structural rather than temporary. A week lets the pool fill, lets a map be learned, and still turns over faster than a community of that size can exhaust it.

The freshness a daily buys is real, and the way to have both is to make it a different mode that does not depend on a pool at all: a daily seeded map against the hand-authored floor and your own stored ghosts, scored on the histogram. That is Slay the Spire's daily exactly, it needs no population, and it works on day one with one player — which nothing else in the multiplayer plan does.

5Three collisions

C1Rotation versus the no-live-service rule Would be fatal

The vision permits exactly one permanent obligation — the server — and rules out live-service cadence in the strongest terms it uses anywhere. A hand-authored map of the week is that cadence wearing a hat. It would convert a project that can be put down and picked up into one with a weekly deadline, which is the specific failure the rule exists to prevent.

Resolved by construction, and only by construction. Generate the archive once, offline, with the harness doing the selecting. Ship the archive. The rotation is then arithmetic on a date — no human in the loop, no deadline, no obligation. This is the whole reason §2 recommends building an archive rather than a generator you invoke weekly, and it is worth being rigid about: the moment a person is choosing the map, the rule has been broken.

C2Rotation versus the ghost pool Open

Covered in §4 and unresolved. Three candidate answers, none free:

Rotate slowly enough that the pool fills — cheapest, and the recommendation above. Generate the hand-authored floor with the map — the sweep already produces good plans as a by-product of scoring, so a map could ship with its own synthetic opponents at every stage; this is attractive and it costs a decision about whether a machine-found defense is a fair opponent. Let the pool cross maps — cheapest to build, worst to play: a defense meets waves on geometry it was never built for, and MapHash exists in the record precisely to stop that happening by accident.

C3Rotation versus mastery A design choice, not a bug

Map knowledge is a skill. Rotating the map deletes it, and with the corridor gone this is a large skill to delete — the geometry axis was just reinstated at considerable cost, and a map that vanishes before anyone learns it gives back part of what that bought.

The lever is archive behaviour rather than cadence: an archive that repeats — a pool of maps that come round again — keeps map knowledge meaningful while still preventing a single map from being ground flat. That is competitive shooters' map-pool logic rather than a roguelike's endless seeds, and it is a different answer from "generate a new one forever". Worth deciding deliberately, because it is invisible until a year in.

6What to build, in order

All of it is shell-runnable and none of it needs the engine, which puts it in the same cheap-to-learn bracket as steps 1 to 4 of the build order.

  1. Make the sweep take its map as a parameter. One signature, and it is the difference between a harness that prices units and one that can also score maps. Do it at step 4, when the harness is built, not after — retrofitting it means touching every call site.
  2. A simcli mode: seed in, map out, hash printed. The smallest possible version of the generator, with a trivial generator behind it. This is the thing that makes everything downstream testable from a shell, and it is what "seeding games really easily" actually means in practice.
  3. Score one map. Sweep N plans against it, report the outcome spread. This is D7 and it is a CSV column. At this point "is this map any good" is answerable without a human.
  4. Then the search. Only now does a real generator pay off, because only now is there something to steer it. Start with random generate-and-filter — keep the candidates whose spread lands in the band — and reach for MAP-Elites when the archive needs to be deliberately diverse rather than merely good.
  5. The scheduler, last and trivially. A date, an archive, a modulus.

Note what this order gets you before the generator exists: after step 3 you can measure whether any map — including a hand-drawn one — has decisions in it. That is the instrument the maze reversal needs regardless of whether rotation ever ships, which is why it belongs at step 4 rather than being bundled into the generation work.

7What could not be verified

  • Every compute claim rests on the inherited 2.75 ms, measured on the committed match: four unit types, one wave, a one-hex corridor, no pathfinding. The maze reversal adds a pathfinder to the hot loop and FlyProjectiles is already O(projectiles × creeps). A generation sweep multiplies whatever the new number is by the candidate count, so this is the claim most likely to be wrong and the one worth re-measuring first.
  • The repository facts were read from source, the design facts were not tested. HexMap.FromCells, HexMap.MapHash, the ulong seed on Match and GhostRecord's MapHash/MapHandle were all confirmed by reading sim/. That they compose into a working generated-map pipeline is an argument, not a demonstration.
  • The PCG material is survey-level. Search-based PCG, its simulation-fitness cost objection, and MAP-Elites were read from abstracts and survey summaries rather than from the primary papers. The direction is well established; specific algorithm choices should be made against the real papers, not against this note.
  • The cadence recommendation has no data behind it. It is reasoning from friend-scale population against a pool that does not exist yet. The curves in §4 are illustrative — nobody can put real numbers on that horizontal axis until people are playing.
  • No shipped tower defense was found doing generated rotating competitive maps, so the combination is untested rather than proven. The parts are all shipped separately: seeded dailies, rotating map pools, and generated TD levels each exist on their own.
