Research note Feeds vision §12 6 August 2026

Making the plan the game,
and spending the simulation

How other games make a preparation phase carry the whole experience, and what a deterministic simulation that resolves a match in 2.75 milliseconds can be spent on as design material rather than as tooling.

Companion to Why tower defense is fun, and where the skill is, which found that every surviving skill axis in this design lands in one phase. This note is about what to put in that phase. It decides nothing.

Bottom line

The games that make a planning phase carry everything all do the same two things: they give away the mechanism completely, and they withhold the outcome. Perfect information about how the world works is what makes a plan a plan; perfect information about how it ends is what makes it data entry.

A 2.75 ms deterministic match is not a faster test suite. It is a mechanic. Fourteen are catalogued below, of which six have no equivalent anywhere in tower defense — the retrospective review, the computed highlight reel, the field distribution, the paid oracle, the measured solvability of a map, and par. The scarce resource in all of them is not compute. It is the player's attention, and the discipline is spending simulation on the three matches that were close rather than on the ninety-seven that were not.

1The genre this actually is

Once the build phase carries all the skill and the resolution carries none, the useful comparisons stop being tower defense games. Four other things are doing this job already, and each has solved a different part of it.

The neighbourWhat it solvedWhat it takes from here
Path of Building Theorycrafting as the activity — a full offline calculator for damage, defences and tree choices The community built it themselves because the game's own tools were worse. This project can ship the planner as the game rather than watch someone else write it
Zachtronics Turning a finished solution into a comparison — histograms across cost, cycles and space Competing metrics, deliberately in tension; and comparison without a leaderboard
Football Manager A match you configure and then watch, with highlight density and a post-match apparatus The entire problem of §4 — how a non-interactive resolution stays worth attending
Into the Breach Perfect information about enemy intent, so a turn becomes a puzzle rather than a gamble The rule about which information is safe to give away — §5

None of them is a tower defense. All of them are planning games with a computed resolution, which is what this design has become.

2Eight ways a plan phase sings

Mechanisms, each with the shipped precedent that proves it works. These are about making the decision good. §3 is about making the information good, and they are different problems.

P1Give away the mechanism entirely Cheap

Into the Breach telegraphs every enemy attack and every damage number; the player knows turn order, resource state and map completely. The designers describe it as a puzzle game wrapped in a strategy game, and the argument for why it works is about blame: when everything was knowable, "the only cause of failure becomes the players themselves", which is what makes a loss feel earned instead of arbitrary.

Nearly nothing, and it is the single highest-value thing on this list. Range overlays, damage previews, the arithmetic done for the player, the purchase shown against what it replaces. It moves difficulty from arithmetic to judgement, which is where it belongs.

P2The public shared offering Cheap · adopted

Mechabellum offers four reinforcements a round — unit, specialist, equipment, skill — take one or skip for supplies, and already-taken cards never return. The load-bearing detail is that both players see the same four; its own community's phrasing is that this "becomes a bit of a mind game". Choosing what your opponent also needs is a strictly better decision than choosing what you need.

A shared seed and an exclusion list. The vision adopted this on 6 August 2026; what remains is the offering's width and whether skipping pays.

P3Scouting, and stale scouting Cheap

Teamfight Tactics makes scouting a taught skill with its own hotkeys: you read boards to decide itemisation, positioning and whether to commit to a composition, and the guidance is explicitly about contest — take the units your opponents need, build the defence their damage type does not beat.

Free in the lobby, where the board is already on the server. The stale variant is the more interesting design: showing the opponent's board as of the previous round prices change, so what they had is evidence rather than truth, and shifting your build to deny a read becomes possible.

P4Metered picks paid for inside the simulation Medium

Element TD meters eleven element picks across fifty waves, and every pick after the first summons a boss you must kill before the element unlocks. The depth research called this the most transferable mechanism in its whole survey and the thing nobody cites about the game: the tech choice is paid for in the match, not chosen in a menu.

A wave type and an unlock gate. It composes directly with the vision's variance anchor schedule — an anchor wave that both unlocks something and demands something is one mechanism, not two.

P5Commit under a clock, resolve without one Cheap

Every simultaneous-turn game in the survey runs a build timer. It is what converts an optimisation problem into a decision — the difference between chess and correspondence chess. It is also the direct antidote to the analysis paralysis that §3's information richness will otherwise cause.

A timer, and in the async mode a deliberate absence of one. The two modes should differ here as they differ on information.

P6The plan is a document you can hold Medium

Path of Building's whole existence is the evidence: players plan in an offline calculator, away from the game, because it gives breakdowns the game does not — and then share the plan as a link. The planning artefact became the community's unit of currency.

This project's records are already content-addressed and self-contained. A build is therefore already a shareable object; what is missing is a screen that treats it as one. Named builds, saved openings, a line you can send a friend.

P7Two things to spend, and they compete Cheap · adopted

The reversal to two purses is what makes the build phase two builds rather than one build and a tax. The competitive precedent is unanimous — Legion TD 2 and Bloons TD Battles 2 both make the attacking purchase return something — and the point for this section is narrower: a phase with one budget has one shape of decision in it, and a phase carrying an entire game needs more than one.

Adopted 6 August 2026. The open part is the cross-feed.

P8Placement that is a decision, not a location Expensive · adopted

The mazing literature is blunt about the size of this: a creep crossing a board in a straight line spends about two seconds under fire, and the same board folded into a switchback puts it under fire for twelve, from both sides, with no extra gold spent. Elevation compounds it — the common shipped form is +1 range per elevation level, which makes where and what one decision.

Real. Deterministic pathfinding in the hot loop, a third coordinate in the record, a format version and a retired ghost pool. Adopted anyway, because it restores the axis the genre was popularised on.

3The sim as design material

BudgetTests times the committed match at 2.75 ms — about 360 matches a second on one core. Everything below follows from taking that number seriously as a content budget rather than a test budget. A run against a hundred stored defenses is under a second. A counterfactual is one match. A thousand-sample forecast is three seconds.

Six of these have no equivalent in tower defense, marked accordingly. That is not because nobody thought of them; it is because a genre built on floating-point, frame-rate-dependent simulations cannot re-run anything and get the same answer twice.

D1A distribution instead of a result No genre equivalent

Your composed wave is run against a sample of the field, and you are told its mean and its spread — not a verdict against one opponent. Reward the best you achieved and the average separately, because peak play and robust play are different skills and a player optimising one will do worse at the other.

That tension is SpaceChem's, deliberately: it shipped three competing metrics because "players optimizing for one criterion often do poorly in the others", which is what stops a single dominant solution. Two metrics in tension is a meta all by itself — a greedy spike build and a boring reliable one can both be correct, and they are not the same player.

A sweep and two columns. It is the cheapest genuinely new thing on this list.

D2Retrospective review — a chess engine for tower defense No genre equivalent

Re-running a finished match with one purchase changed costs one match. So after a run, every build phase can be re-resolved against every alternative that was affordable at the time, and the swing reported: wave 7, the sniper instead of the tank was worth fourteen points of clear probability. Move classification, an accuracy figure, a marked blunder.

Chess.com's Game Review is the model and also the warning — its accuracy metric is contested, has been silently recalibrated, and occasionally decorates ordinary moves as brilliant. The lesson is to report the swing and let the player judge it, rather than to grade them with a number whose derivation they cannot see.

Compute is trivial. The design cost is real: this is the most powerful teaching tool available here and the fastest possible route to a solved meta, because a tool that names the best move every time eventually publishes the book. Ship it for your own runs; think hard before ranking with it.

D3The computed highlight reel No genre equivalent

Because the whole match resolves before anything is drawn, the moments worth watching can be chosen rather than recorded. Salience is a computation over a finished match: the tick where the outcome was closest to flipping, the first leak, the largest single swing in remaining health, the shot with the highest marginal contribution to the result.

This is the single most valuable consequence of determinism for presentation, and it inverts the normal relationship between a camera and a match. Nothing has to be captured. The director knows the ending.

Football Manager 26's Dynamic Highlight Mode does the human version — it varies how many highlights it shows based on the context of the match, and lets the viewer pick extended action or key moments only. It cannot compute salience, because its match engine is not re-runnable. This one can.

A salience function over an existing event stream, and a camera that reads it.

D4The paid oracle No genre equivalent

Simulation offered to the player as a resource. Three test runs per build phase; a fourth costs gold. A cheap forecast returns a coarse answer — likely to clear — and an expensive one returns the distribution. Suddenly how much do I need to know before I commit? is a purchase competing with a tower.

This is the most interesting idea in the note and the one with no precedent at all, because no other game can afford to sell simulation by the unit. It also solves §5 by construction: the forecast is partial because the player chose not to pay for more of it.

A currency sink, a sampling rule, and a UI that shows an uncertain answer honestly. Design risk is analysis paralysis; P5's clock is the counterweight.

D5Placement against the aggregate Cheap

Your result shown as a histogram against everybody who reached the same stage, rather than as a rank.

SpaceChem replaced global leaderboards with histograms for two stated reasons, both of which apply here exactly. Cheating: "getting your name at the top of the leaderboards is a fantastic incentive for cheating" — and a server that re-simulates every claim has already paid for the other half of that problem. And morale: "for most players, the only thing a global leaderboard manages to tell you is that you suck (and not even by how much)." Zachtronics found players set themselves improvement targets off the histogram unprompted.

A count per bucket per stage. Trivially small, and it composes with D1's two metrics.

D6Par, computed No genre equivalent

The harness can sweep a stage and report what the best line it found achieves. That is a par — a difficulty rating derived from the game rather than asserted about it, refreshed automatically when content changes, and available on day one when no player population exists to derive it from.

A sweep mode and a search. The honest caveat is that a harness's best line is only as good as its search, so par is a floor on difficulty rather than a truth.

D7Solvability, measured No genre equivalent

"A maze that is far less solvable" is a design goal that sounds like taste and is not. Sweep many good plans against a map and report the spread of outcomes across them. A map where every competent plan scores the same is solved. A map where competent plans diverge has decisions in it.

This is the instrument the maze reversal needs, and it should exist before the map content does — the same argument that pulled the balance harness forward to step 4.

The existing sweep, pointed at maps instead of units. One more CSV column.

D8The eval bar Medium

Chess's win-probability strip, for a wave. Re-simulate forward from each tick against the sampled field and draw the result as a curve under the board — so a wave that looked calm but was one unit from collapsing looks like that.

Real compute during watch, and a genuine hazard: an eval bar tells the viewer the ending early and can drain the tension it was meant to show. Better as a post-match overlay than a live one.

D9The ghost of your own best Cheap

Trackmania's medal ghost, applied to a stage: your own best defense at that stage, stored, as an opponent. It gives the solo player a graded ladder with no service behind it, and it gives the pool a useful floor of hand-shaped opponents at every stage — which the cold-start problem needs anyway.

Nothing new. A ghost record is a ghost record.

D10The position as a puzzle you can send Cheap

Records are content-addressed and self-contained, so any position is already a portable challenge: here is the board and the budget I had at wave 12 — beat my result. A daily seeded stage is the same object with a schedule attached, and it is Slay the Spire's daily, which the vision already cites for a different reason.

A share button and a route. The format work is done.

D11Counterfactual scrubbing in the plan phase Medium

Seeking already re-simulates rather than caching, which means the machinery for "show me this again with one thing different" is present. Hovering a purchase could show the wave you are about to face resolved both ways, side by side.

Interface, mostly. Also the closest thing here to the oracle problem in §5 — this is the feature that most needs to be sampled or paid for rather than free.

D12Commentary derived from events Cheap

Match events are already decorative and already emitted. A line of text attached to the three salient moments — "the third wall held for four ticks longer than it needed to" — is the cheapest possible presence layer, and presence is what the social seam exists to manufacture.

A template table over an event stream that exists.

D13Attribution — which purchase actually did the work Medium

Damage dealt per tower is a DPS meter and mostly a lie, because it rewards whoever landed the last hit on something that was already dying. The re-runnable version is honest: remove one tower and re-simulate. The difference is that tower's actual contribution, and it will disagree with the damage numbers in ways that teach.

One match per tower. On a twelve-tower board that is 33 ms.

D14The sweep as a content check, not a balance check Cheap

The companion note's warning applies here too: a sweep finds outliers, and removing outliers repeatedly converges on a roster where nothing matters. The target is not a flat win-rate table but one where every unit has both a strong and a weak column — a unit with no bad matchup is as much a failure as one with no good matchup.

Two columns on a CSV that already exists.

4The non-interactive run

The criticism is real and it is levelled at every auto-battler: the player places units and then watches a fight they cannot affect, and the standard complaint is that battles need more to look at and more to decide. The genre's own answer is circular — make the preparation good enough that the resolution is satisfying to watch — and it is only half true. The other half is that the resolution has a job, and the job is to pay the player for having thought.

Five things to do with the watch phase

  • Do not show ninety-seven of them. Resolve the field instantly, then spend the watching time only on the matches that were close — the two that came down to one unit, and the one you lost worst. This is the whole idea, and it needs D3 to pick them.
  • Let the viewer set the density. Football Manager 26 varies highlight count by match context and lets the viewer choose extended or key-moments-only. A run that is going well should be watchable in twenty seconds; a run on a knife edge should be watchable in five minutes. Same match, same record.
  • Show the near-miss explicitly. The most interesting fact about a wave you cleared is often how nearly you did not. That is a computable quantity — how much less would have been needed — and displaying it converts a boring win into a story.
  • Make the dashboard the destination, not the consolation. Mean and spread against the field, placement histogram, per-tower true contribution from D13, and the two rewards from D1. If the watching is optional, the dashboard has to be worth arriving at on its own.
  • Keep the scrub. Seeking already re-simulates, which means the player can already rewind and answer why did that happen at their own pace. That is a rare capability and it turns the resolution from a cutscene into an inspectable object.

Nothing here asks for in-wave input, so none of it threatens the submission barrier or the stored ghost. The run stays non-interactive; what changes is that it stops being a fixed-length video and becomes a queryable result with a recommended viewing.

5The oracle problem

The one real hazard

A simulation that answers everything deletes the game.

Every mechanism in §3 gives the player more certainty before they commit. Taken to its limit that is not a strategy game; it is data entry against a solver. The failure would arrive quietly and look like a feature the whole way in.

Into the Breach is the calibration point and the distinction is exact: it gives perfect information about mechanism — what each enemy will do next turn, what every number is — and none at all about outcome. You know precisely what happens if you do X. You do not know whether the run survives.

That yields a usable rule, and it is the thing this note most wants to hand to seam 1:

Kind of informationGive it awayWhy
Mechanism Freely Ranges, damage, armour, tick order, what a unit does. Hiding these buys difficulty made of ignorance, which is the worst kind
The field Sampled Mean and spread against many defenses, never the specific opponents. Uncertainty comes from breadth, not from dice
A specific outcome Paid or never "Does this exact wave beat that exact defense" is the solver query. Sell it (D4) or withhold it
The best line After, not during D2's review is a teaching tool in retrospect and a cheat sheet in advance. The tense is the whole design

The design already contains its own antidote, and it should be recognised as one. The round-robin measures a wave against many defenses, so the honest answer to what does this do? is a distribution rather than a number — not because anything is hidden or random, but because the question has many answers. That is the only form of uncertainty compatible with a deterministic, re-simulable, server-verifiable game, and it is worth protecting deliberately rather than by accident.

Three smaller risks, named

  • Analysis paralysis. Rich information plus no clock is a build phase that takes eleven minutes. P5's timer is the counterweight, and the async mode deliberately not having one is a choice that should be made rather than defaulted into.
  • The review publishes the book. D2 is the fastest route to a solved meta anyone has ever handed a community. Shipping it for your own runs is a teaching tool; wiring it into the ladder is a speedrun to the one correct opening.
  • Dashboards can crowd out the game. Everything in §3 is an interface, and the skill note's two-board finding already says attention is the constraint. A screen that shows all fourteen is worse than one that shows three.

The two decisions this note would put in front of seam 1

  1. What can the player compute before committing, and what does it cost them? This is now question 5 on the step 1 checklist. The answer determines whether §3 is a set of mechanics or a solver.
  2. Is the round-robin's reward the best, the average, or both? Both is the interesting answer and it is SpaceChem's, but it defines what the ladder measures — and therefore what everyone optimises — so it is not a UI decision.

7What could not be verified

  • The 2.75 ms figure is inherited, not re-measured here, and every compute claim in §3 is arithmetic on it. It was measured on the committed match, on the development laptop, with four unit types and one wave. A maze, pathfinding, elevation and a ten-unit roster will all make it worse, and FlyProjectiles is already known to be O(projectiles × creeps). Nothing here should be planned against 2.75 ms without re-measuring after the maze lands.
  • Mechabellum's card system was read from community wikis and guides, not from the game. The load-bearing claim — that both players see the same reinforcement options — comes from a player description rather than a developer statement, and it should be confirmed before the shared offering is built on it.
  • "No genre equivalent" is a claim about what searching found, across tower defense and auto-battlers. It is a weaker claim than it sounds: a mechanic can exist in a game nobody wrote about.
  • D4, the paid oracle, has no precedent at all, which is the reason it is interesting and the reason it is ranked ninth rather than first. Nothing in this survey tells you whether selling simulation by the unit is fun; only a prototype does.
  • The Football Manager and chess.com material came from search extracts and support pages rather than from playing either. The highlight-density and review-accuracy claims are directionally safe and numerically unverified.
