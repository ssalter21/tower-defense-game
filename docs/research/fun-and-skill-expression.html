Why tower defense is fun,
and where the skill is

A survey of what the genre's durable games actually charge the player for, and an audit of which of those charges this design is still able to make.

Scope: the two questions as asked — why the genre is fun, and where its skill expression lives — then the same taxonomy run past this project's settled decisions. It decides nothing. It is a decision input for seams 1, 3 and 7.

Bottom line

Tower defense is fun because it converts a plan into a spectacle you are the author of. The plan is the move; the wave is the verdict; and unlike almost every other strategy genre, the verdict is legible enough that losing teaches you something.

Skill in the genre comes from eight structurally distinct axes. This design deletes two of them outright, inverts a third, and leaves a fourth with nothing to attach to. The four that survive are real and they are enough for a game — but every one of them is collected in the build phase, so a design that spreads skill across two phases in every shipped comparison spends all of it in one here.

1Why it is fun

Six mechanisms recur across every game in the survey. They are not a list of features; they are the reasons the loop holds a person. The seventh entry is the same list read backwards, because each of these properties has an inverse that is exactly how tower defense games fail.

1.1The plan is the move, and the wave is the verdict

The defining structure of the genre is that your input and its consequence are separated in time. You commit, then you stop, then you find out. Nothing else in strategy games does this so cleanly — an RTS lets you correct mid-engagement, a card game resolves your move immediately.

The pleasure this produces is authorship of an automation: the towers do the killing, but the thing that killed was designed by you thirty seconds ago. It is the same satisfaction as a factory line or a good regex — a machine acting out your intent without further instruction. It is also why the genre survives at very low input rates, and why an idle-adjacent subgenre grew out of it rather than out of anything else.

1.2Losing is legible

When you lose a wave you can point at the leak. The unit that got through, the tower that could not reach it, the gold you spent on the wrong thing. Very few genres offer a post-mortem that specific; in most, defeat arrives as a general sense of having been outplayed.

This is what makes a retry feel like a correction rather than another roll of the dice, and it is the quiet engine under the genre's famous replay compulsion. It also raises the presentation stakes: an illegible loss converts the genre's best property into its worst.

1.3A wave table is a curriculum

Koster's claim — that fun is what learning feels like from the inside, and that a game dies at the moment its pattern is fully learned — describes tower defense unusually well, because a wave schedule is a syllabus with a fixed order.

Games get boring when the player has learned the pattern and there is nothing new to learn.

A Theory of Fun for Game Design — Raph Koster

Bloons TD 6 makes this concrete: in its restricted mode the difficulty is carried by a handful of memorised landmarks — camo at round 24, lead at 28, the first MOAB at 40, grouped ceramics at 63, fortified MOABs at 98. The skill being tested is not reaction. It is having prepared for round 40 on round 30.

1.4Two curves, racing

Your defense compounds; the threat escalates. The tension in a run is entirely the gap between those two curves, and the fun is that the gap is readable in advance — you can feel a wave coming that you are not ready for, several waves before it arrives. Dread is a design output here, not an accident.

1.5Few actions, heavy actions

The genre's mass appeal comes from an unusual ratio: a small number of decisions, each of which matters a lot. Plants vs. Zombies is the canonical proof — its designer's stated goal was to strip tower defense down until anyone could play it, keeping the field small and cutting the conventions that were unintuitive rather than load-bearing, with each unit's function readable off its silhouette.

This is the same claim the vision's accessibility pillar makes, and the survey supports it: legibility is not a concession that costs depth. It is what lets depth be perceived at all.

1.6The spectacle is the payment

The wave is not dead time between decisions. It is the reward the decision was made for. Every durable game in the genre spends heavily here — impact, numbers, death weight — because the watching phase is where the player is paid for having thought.

1.7And each of those inverts

The genre's failure modes are not separate from its pleasures; they are the same properties with the sign flipped. Deferred agency becomes passivity — the standing critique is that if the player acts once every thirty seconds the game is mostly watching and waiting. Legible causality becomes an obvious answer, and a level with one correct solution is a chore, not a puzzle. A learned curriculum becomes a memorised script. Two racing curves become a solved arithmetic problem. Few heavy actions become no actions.

Every design decision in the sections below should be read as choosing which side of this line it lands on. There is no third option.

2The eight skill axes

A game's skill expression is the set of things a strong player does that a weak player does not. Across the survey those reduce to eight, and they are structurally distinct: each can be present or absent independently of the others, and each fails in its own way.

For each: what it tests, the strongest shipped evidence that it is real, and what this project's settled decisions do to it.

S1Geometry — placement, coverage, mazing Deleted

Getting more shots out of the same gold by controlling where the fighting happens.

The purest expression of it, and the one the genre was popularised on. Desktop Tower Defense (2007) was built specifically because its author found no game that let you properly maze; it was played over 15 million times inside four months. The skill is quantifiable: a creep crossing a board in a straight line spends about two seconds under fire, and the same board folded into a switchback puts it under fire for twelve — a sixfold damage multiplier bought with no gold at all. Two players with identical towers and identical gold post wildly different results purely on how they folded the path.

Gone, permanently and deliberately. The corridor is one hex wide and never branches, so no unit chooses a path and no placement lengthens one. This is the single largest deletion in the design and it is already settled. What remains of the axis is a thin residue — which hexes along a fixed corridor a unit covers — and that is a range number, not a spatial skill.

S2Wave knowledge — knowing what is coming Nothing to attach to

Preparation as a skill: holding the schedule in your head and buying for a threat that is not on screen yet.

Bloons TD 6's restricted mode is almost entirely this axis. The round landmarks are public, memorised, and identical every attempt; the mode's difficulty comes from removing every safety net — no continues, no income, no powers, no selling — so a purchase made forty rounds early has to have been correct. Element TD's fixed public wave list does the same work, which is why its element picks read as commitments rather than shopping.

The mechanism is available but the object it needs does not exist yet. Here the player composes the whole opposing wave, so there is no shared constant to prepare against and no schedule to memorise. This is the vision's open baseline wave question — and this survey reframes it. It is not cheap insurance for legibility. Without a public constant, an entire skill axis has nothing to be a skill about.

S3Economy and tempo — greed against safety At risk

Choosing between strength now and strength later, repeatedly, under threat.

This is the axis competitive tower defense is actually built on, and both surviving PvP games in the genre make the same structural move: they make the attack purchase pay you back. In Legion TD 2, gold buys workers, workers make mythium at 1 per 10 seconds, mythium buys mercenaries, and income rises permanently whenever mythium is spent — so every send is an investment. In Bloons TD Battles 2 the same idea is compressed onto a single continuous dial: sends grant eco, eco pays out every six seconds, and the low tiers pay best while the high tiers cost you income to throw. Sending is simultaneously the attack and the economy, and the whole skill is knowing which of the two you are buying this round.

Live, and this is where one purse bites. The vision already flags it: under a single currency a coin spent attacking is simply gone, so attacking is a pure tempo loss and at equilibrium dominated. Restated as skill expression the problem is sharper than a balance problem — if attacking never pays back, there is no dial, and a dial is the thing players get good at. The outcome transfer the sending research recommends is not merely the cheapest fix; it is the one that restores the axis rather than flattening it.

S4Risk budgeting — spending your health Live

Treating the loss condition as a currency with an exchange rate, not a wall to stand behind.

The clearest statement of it in the genre comes from Legion TD 2's advanced play, where deliberately conceding a wave to fund a stronger position later is standard: "King HP is a resource, to a certain extent. Sacrificing some HP to generate a better pressure point against your opponent can be valuable if properly executed." The corresponding beginner error is trying to clear every wave — which is to say, valuing the resource at infinity and therefore never spending it.

Live, cheap, and largely free. It needs a graded loss condition rather than a binary one — something that can be spent in fractions. This lands squarely on the vision's open what is a run question, and the answer with the most skill expression in it is a health pool deep enough that losing a wave on purpose can be correct.

S5Reading the opponent — inference under hidden information Inverted

Working out what the other player has, what they will do, and what they think you have.

In Legion TD 2 this is an explicit, taught skill on both sides of the screen: identifying an opponent's build and their weak waves, then timing pressure onto them — and equally, concealing your own line. Its advanced guide is blunt that "hiding information in Legion TD 2 can be quite an effective weapon", and describes shifting a build precisely to deny a read.

Not deleted — inverted, and this is the finding this note most wants to hand to seam 1. A stored ghost is a frozen, fully-inspectable opponent. There is no bluff, no shift, no concealment, because the defense you are composing against cannot react and cannot lie. Perfect information about a static target does not produce reading; it produces optimisation. That is a real skill, but it is a different one — closer to a puzzle solve — and it degrades toward a lookup as soon as the roster is small enough to memorise. The sending research named this exact failure (counter-picking collapsing to a lookup) as a hazard of gating; the ghost causes it independently of any gating decision.

S6Composition — synergy and combinatorics Live · load-bearing

Knowing which pieces multiply each other, and committing to an identity before you know what you will face.

Element TD is the vision's named anchor and the survey agrees with the choice, but with an important correction the depth research already landed: the combination table is what makes towers memorable, while the metered element picks are what make a run a decision. Super Auto Pets is the other clean case — its skill is almost entirely composition, played against stored opponents, and it is the closest structural relative this design has.

Live, and it now has to carry more weight than the vision assumed. With S1 and S8 deleted, S5 inverted and S2 unanchored, composition is not one axis among eight — it is the majority of the remaining skill surface. The vision's instinct to spend the depth budget here is correct; what this note adds is that it is not optional.

S7Sequencing — order and timing Live

Same pieces, different order, different outcome.

Usually the genre's weakest axis, because in most games ordering is diluted by routing — a wave that can split across paths does not really have an order. The exceptions prove the rule: where the lane is single-file, order becomes a genuine lever, deciding which unit soaks the opening volley and which arrives while a cooldown is down.

Live, strengthened, and already half-built. The one-hex corridor that deletes S1 is precisely what rescues S7 — a corridor that never branches is a single-file column, so there is no routing left to dilute the order. The sending research reached the same conclusion independently and recorded the two preconditions the skeleton already found: order is unobservable when units share a speed, and unobservable again when a count spawns as one pile.

S8Execution — acting during the wave Deleted

Ability timing, target priority, and the manual interventions that rescue a wave in progress.

Present in most of the genre and load-bearing in some of it: ability chaining in Bloons TD 6, targeting priority toggles, and at the extreme, Desktop Tower Defense's juggling — deliberately opening and sealing alternate exits to walk a wave back and forth through the same maze indefinitely.

Gone by design, and the deletion is correct. Nothing happens during a wave; that is what makes a stored ghost a valid opponent and a submission barrier a substitute for netcode. The whole multiplayer architecture rests on it. It is not reopenable and should not be reopened — but its cost should be stated in the same breath, and it is the subject of §4.

4What this design is left holding

Finding 1 · structural

Every surviving axis is collected in the same phase.

In every comparison game, skill is spread across two phases: you plan between waves and you act during them. This design has no during. S8 is deleted outright, and S1 — the other axis that classically pays off while the wave is running — is deleted too. Everything that is left is submitted before the wave starts and is then unalterable.

That is a coherent architecture and it is what buys the whole multiplayer plan. But it means the build phase is not a place skill lives; it is the place, and it has to be as dense as two phases were elsewhere. A build phase that offers three clicks and a confirm button is not a smaller version of Legion TD 2's build phase. It is the entire game.

Finding 2 · the ghost

The async ghost turns the genre's richest PvP skill into a lookup.

Reading an opponent is inference under uncertainty. A stored defense removes the uncertainty: it is visible, static, and cannot respond to being read. What is left is an optimisation problem with a known input — genuinely skilful the first fifty times, and then a table.

The vision already anticipated the symptom in a different context, warning that counter-picking could collapse to a lookup if the ghost is shown before composition. This note's contribution is that the cause is not the gating idea. It is the ghost itself, and it applies whichever way seam 1 decides gating.

Three mitigations exist and they are all cheap, because they are ordering rules over a record that already exists — compose before the draw, show a summary rather than a layout, or commit and then reveal. All three trade the optimisation puzzle back for a genuine read. None of them needs an engine, and the right time to choose is step 6, when opponent defenses first come from a folder.

Finding 3 · one purse

The economy axis is not merely hard to balance — without a payback it is not an axis.

The vision treats one purse as a balance problem with three known answers. Read through skill expression it is a bigger claim than that. Both surviving competitive tower defense games put their highest skill ceiling on the same lever, and in both cases the lever exists because the attack purchase returns something: permanent income in Legion TD 2, eco per six seconds in Bloons TD Battles 2. A purchase that only ever subtracts has no timing question attached to it, and timing is what players practise.

This does not argue against one purse. It argues that step 1's payback decision is the single highest-leverage decision in the entire build order for skill expression, higher than the roster, and it is due on the day the purse is added rather than after.

Finding 4 · the baseline

With no public wave, there is nothing for preparation to be preparation about.

A memorised schedule is a skill. It is also, in Legion TD 2 and Element TD alike, the constant that makes everything else legible: a send reads as aggressive only against a wave both players already know. Here the player composes the entire wave, so the constant is absent and S2 has no object.

The vision lists the baseline wave as an open question blocking step 1, justified as cheap insurance for a newcomer's legibility. The stronger justification is that it is the only thing that makes preparation a skill at all, and it doubles as the fix for Finding 2 — a public constant is something to read a send against without needing to inspect an opponent's board.

5Where the fun could fail

§1's six mechanisms, run past this design's specific commitments. Three of them are at risk in ways that are particular to this game rather than to the genre.

Two boards halve the payment

§1.6 says the watching phase is where the player is paid. This design runs two resolutions at once and asks the player to watch both. Attention is not additive: a player watching two boards sees each of them about half as well, and the moment that pays — your tower killing that unit because you bought it three waves ago — is exactly the moment that gets lost when the eye is elsewhere.

The vision already names the two-board interface as the hardest unsolved problem in the design, on legibility grounds. This adds a second reason, and it argues for the boards being staged rather than simultaneous — resolve one, then the other — unless the interface work proves that simultaneity reads.

An absent opponent has no face

§1.2 says legible loss is the genre's best property, but a legible loss to nobody is thinner than a legible loss to a person. Super Auto Pets is the direct precedent and it works, which is reassuring; the vision's social layer seam exists for exactly this reason and should be read as a fun requirement rather than a nicety.

A computed roster tends to flat

The balance harness is a genuine asset and the vision's argument for it holds. The risk it carries for fun is specific: a sweep finds outliers, and the natural response to a red cell is to remove the outlier. Repeat that enough times and you converge on a roster where everything is equally good — which is to say, where composition does not matter, which deletes S6.

The vision already has half of this in hand — it notes that depth and computed balance are the same axis pointing opposite ways. The practical version for the harness is that the target is not a flat win-rate table. It is a table where every unit has both a strong and a weak column. A unit with no bad matchup is as much a failure as one with no good matchup, and the sweep can report both from the same CSV.

6What to measure, and when

The build order asks is this fun first, deliberately, and steps 1 to 4 run from a shell. The useful observation is that a sweep harness pointed at skill expression rather than at balance answers several of the questions above without an engine and without a player.

Three measurements the harness can already produce

  1. Strategy spread. Sweep every composition against every stored defense and report the win-rate range across compositions. A narrow range means the decisions do not matter — the S6 failure in §5 — and it is visible long before a human would feel it.
  2. Order sensitivity. Take one wave, permute its send order, and report the outcome spread. If permuting the order changes nothing, S7 is not live yet, and the two known preconditions (shared speeds, piled counts) are the first place to look. This is a direct test of a claimed axis and it costs a loop.
  3. Both-columns check. For each unit, report its best and worst matchup rather than its mean. A unit with no weak column is the flatness failure; a unit with no strong column is dead weight. Same CSV, one extra pair of columns.

Two decisions this note would move earlier

  • The baseline wave, from open question to step 1. It already blocks step 1 in the vision. Finding 4 upgrades the reason: it is not insurance, it is the anchor an entire skill axis hangs from, and it is a text file.
  • Ghost-reveal ordering, at step 6. Whether the opponent's defense is visible before or after composition is a one-line rule with a large effect on whether S5 is a read or a lookup. It costs nothing to decide at the moment defenses first come from a folder, and a great deal to revisit after a pool exists.

7What could not be verified

  • Sourcing depth is uneven. The Legion TD 2 manual and advanced guide, the mazing guide, the Stardock design journal, the Kingdom Rush level analysis, the Positech post and the reinforcement-learning paper were read directly. The Bloons TD 6 round landmarks, the Bloons TD Battles 2 eco figures, the Desktop Tower Defense history, the Plants vs. Zombies design account and the Koster summary come from search result extracts rather than the primary pages, and the eco numbers in particular should be re-checked against the wiki before any of them is used to set a rate.
  • No GDC talk or first-party postmortem on tower defense design was found. Searches for Ironhide, Defense Grid and Anomaly returned nothing of that kind. The designer-side sources here are blog posts and dev journals, which is a weaker class of evidence than the vision's other notes rest on.
  • The eight-axis taxonomy is mine, not a citation. It is a reading of the survey, and the boundaries between S3 and S4, and between S6 and S2, are arguable. It is offered because it makes the audit in §3 possible, not because the genre agrees on it.
  • Nothing here was tested in this build. Every claim about what this design deletes is read off the vision and the settled decisions, not off play. The three measurements in §6 exist precisely because none of this has been observed.
